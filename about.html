Theoretical biology

I use mathematical models to make biological assumptions explicit and uncover consequences that intuition alone would not reveal.

Research

I am a theoretical evolutionary biologist. I study how interactions among organisms alter the conditions under which natural selection acts, and how the resulting feedbacks shape cooperation, conflict, persistence, and biological organisation.

My research is organised around three connected problems.

01 Interdependence and cooperation

How can interactions among organisms generate increasingly strong forms of biological dependence? I study how assortment, population structure, ecological dependence, and transmission affect the evolution of cooperation and mutualism, and how these processes can contribute to division of labour and new forms of biological organisation.

02 Conflict and coevolution

Interacting organisms continually modify one another’s selective environments. I study how processes such as sensing, defence, exploitation, and escape reshape evolutionary landscapes, and when these interactions produce stable outcomes, alternative evolutionary states, or continuing evolutionary change.

03 Ecological and evolutionary feedbacks

Evolution takes place within ecological systems that evolving organisms themselves modify. I develop models that couple population and evolutionary dynamics to investigate how these feedbacks affect coexistence, persistence, stability, and the organisation of ecological systems.

Approach

My work is primarily theoretical and combines mathematical analysis with computer simulation. Depending on the biological problem, I use tools from dynamical systems, adaptive dynamics, evolutionary game theory, stochastic modelling, network theory, and individual-based simulation.

The method is secondary to the question. I aim to construct models that are simple enough to expose the mechanism responsible for a result, while retaining the biological structure needed to distinguish alternative explanations and generate testable predictions.

I also collaborate with empirical researchers when theory can help distinguish mechanisms, sharpen experimental questions, or connect observations to broader ecological and evolutionary processes.

Students and collaborations

I welcome enquiries from students interested in theoretical ecology and evolution, including those whose previous training has been primarily biological. A project normally begins with a biological question rather than with a preferred mathematical or computational technique. The methods can then be developed according to what that question requires.

Previous experience with mathematics or programming is useful, but familiarity with a particular method or programming language is less important than curiosity, independence, and a willingness to engage seriously with formal reasoning. Students interested in discussing a possible project are welcome to contact me with a short description of their interests and the questions they would like to explore.

I am also interested in collaborations in which mathematical theory can complement experimental, observational, or comparative work.

I aim to create a research environment in which people with different backgrounds and scientific perspectives can contribute fully. I particularly welcome enquiries from people who have been underrepresented in theoretical and quantitative biology.
